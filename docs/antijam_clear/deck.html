02

The problem

Keep the signal, lose the jammer

Everything reduces to one ratio.

x = e_s·s + e_j·j + n % what each element receives y = w'·x % what the beamformer outputs SINR = σs²|w'e_s|² / ( σj²|w'e_j|² + σn²‖w‖² )

Three statements about the pattern the weights produce: gain towards the signal, gain towards the jammer, and how much noise gets through.

The job is to shrink the second and third without touching the first. e(θ,φ) — the array's response to a wave from one direction — is the only physical primitive; it is read from the measured patterns, so every result is a statement about this hardware and not about an idealised array.

03

Two approaches

Explicit, or implicit

1  Detect and null

Work out where the jammer is and what it is doing, then put a zero in the radiation pattern there.

C = [e_s , e_j] g = [1 ; 0] w = C·(CᴴC)⁻¹·g

No covariance in it at all. Two directions, one closed-form solve. Nothing to converge, nothing to invert from data. It fits on a whiteboard.

Among all weights that hold the signal and kill the jammer, it picks the smallest — and since noise out is σn²‖w‖², smallest is best.

2  Maximise SINR

Never ask where the jammer is. Solve directly for the weights that let through as little non-signal power as possible.

R_loaded = R + δ·I w = R_loaded⁻¹·e_s / (e_sᴴ·R_loaded⁻¹·e_s)

Minimum variance: among all w with unit gain on the signal, minimise total output power. The signal is pinned, so minimising the total minimises everything else.

It cannot be wrong about an angle, because it never estimates one.

The guard sector is derived from the array's own beamwidth — half the wider HPBW. A jammer closer than that is inside the main lobe, and nulling it cancels your own signal. The true value spans 12.6° to 82.8° here, so no single constant can be right.

The mirror ambiguity is tested on the vectors, not the angles. On some arrays e(θ) and e(180−θ) are the same vector to six decimals — harmless when nulling, ruinous when tracking a direction over time.

05

Knowing its own limits

Two arrays that provably cannot do this

Dipole (1 element, 0 degrees of freedom) "Telling a jammer from the noise needs more elements than sources, i.e. at least 3 for a signal and a jammer. Direction finding is not possible here. Quiescent beam." patch_back2back (2 elements) "The array is 113.3 dB down at the target: that is a pattern null, not a beam. It cannot receive its own signal there, so this is not a test."

Neither crashes. Neither returns a meaningless number. Each says what is wrong and falls back to the plain beam.

An N-element array has N weights; one holds the signal, leaving N−1 for nulls. At N = 1 that is zero, and no null exists at any angle. Separately, patch_back2back simply does not radiate co-pol towards this target — so no beamformer, including the perfect-knowledge oracle, can achieve anything there.

A method that reports its own limits precisely is easier to trust than one that always returns a number.
06

The data

One constant explains most of the behaviour

R = λ·R_previous + (1−λ)·(X·Xᴴ)/K a block k steps old keeps weight (1−λ)·λᵏ 1/(1−λ) = 10 steps % effective window — how much smoothing λ/(1−λ) = 9 steps % mean age of the data — the LAG

The array remembers about ten steps. That explains why an on/off edge cannot be resolved from this covariance, and how long a null survives the jammer switching off.

But two different summaries of that memory matter, and they differ by exactly one step. Nothing ever leaves the average — old data just fades. The window length says how much smoothing this is; the mean age says how stale the answer is. Memory is governed by the first; lag is governed by the second, because the beamscan reports where the jammer was on average.

Confirmed by measurement — and it settles which one. Dividing the measured lag by the drift rate gives the lag in steps directly: at 0.10 and 0.20°/step it comes out at 9.00 steps exactly, the mean age. Leading by 10 instead over-shoots by one step's motion every step. On a stationary jammer the estimator is exact to 0.00°.

Snapshots per step is derived too: K = 2N, the count at which a sample covariance lands within 3 dB of the ideal. 32 snapshots for 16 elements.

07

Approach 1, step one

The eigenvalues say whether; a power scan says where

Whether

A covariance of a few strong sources plus noise has a handful of large eigenvalues — one per source — above a flat noise floor. Count how many rise above it: two means signal plus jammer, one means signal alone.

The threshold is the Marchenko–Pastur edge, (1+√(N/K))² — derived from the array size and snapshot count, not tuned.

Where

Sweep a unit-length steering vector across the sky and measure the power the array would receive pointing that way. Largest value outside the guard sector is the jammer.

P(θ,φ) = e_unitᴴ·R·e_unit

Unit norm matters. With raw pattern vectors the scan reports where the array hears best rather than where the jammer is — a 77° error that looked entirely plausible.

The guard sector is what makes this work. The wanted signal is inside the snapshots, so it is one of the strong sources and the scan finds it too — usually as the larger peak. Masking the guard sector is exactly what makes "largest remaining peak" mean "the jammer". Without it, Approach 1 nulls your own signal, confidently and precisely.

A result in its own right

The textbook estimator is the wrong one here

MUSIC is sharper on a stationary source. On a moving one it sticks and jumps.

estimatormedian lagmean |lag|max |lag|behaviour
MUSIC3.50°5.03°13.50°sticks, then jumps
beamscan4.50°4.65°6.00°smooth
 spacing0.6, drifting 0.5°/step. On a stationary jammer the two agree exactly, both to 0.00°.

Why. A jammer smeared across ten steps of memory is no longer a point source — the source count reads 3 on 91 of 120 steps. MUSIC makes its noise subspace orthogonal to the jammer's whole track, its spectrum goes near-singular along a broad arc, and the peak inside that arc is numerically arbitrary.

The beamscan's lag is exactly rate × mean age — a predictable error, which the next slide removes. MUSIC's 13.5° excursion cannot be corrected for.

Switching the null to the beamscan took the drift case from 7.5 dB / score 66 to 11.1 dB / score 99 against an achievable 11.5 — identical settings, identical noise, changing only which spectrum feeds the null. Both estimators are still computed and reported, so the comparison is an output rather than a claim.
09

Approach 1, step two

Decide what it is doing, then aim

Each behaviour gets the aiming rule that suits it, and each rule is one sentence.

  • Not moving → null the median of the window. It is not going anywhere, so average out the jitter.
  • Comes and goes → hold where you last saw it. The null costs almost nothing while the jammer is off and is already in place when it returns.
  • Moving steadily → lead it.
aim = latest estimate + (fitted rate) × (mean age of the covariance) λ/(1−λ) = 9 steps, NOT 1/(1−λ) = 10 capped at the guard sector — half a beamwidth

Both quantities on the right are already known. The rate is the slope we fit; the mean age follows from λ. Nothing is fitted to performance, there is no coefficient to choose, and if λ changes the lead follows automatically.

On the demo scenario it takes aim error from −4.50° to −0.37°, and mean absolute error from 4.65° to 0.75°.

The cap costs no new constant — it reuses the guard sector. The lead multiplies the fitted rate by nine, so it amplifies any error in that rate ninefold. Beyond half a beamwidth ahead there is no measurement bearing on where the jammer will be: do not aim further than you can see.

Approach 2

Diagonal loading is structural, not a knob

The wanted signal is inside the covariance. A receiver cannot remove what it is trying to receive.

If the assumed signal direction is even slightly wrong, the solver sees wanted signal arriving from a direction it is not protecting, correctly calls it "power that is not the signal", and cancels your own transmission.

The honest part

What this simpler version gives up

The lead correction is arithmetic, not a filter. It pays while the fitted rate is well measured, and stops when it is not.

This version is qualified to roughly 0.5°/s and broken by 1.5°/s. The full implementation, using a constant-velocity Kalman filter on [θ, θ̇, φ, φ̇], was qualified to about 10°/s — an envelope some 20× wider. That filter is worth +35 points on drifting jammers and is provably inert on everything else. It is also the single largest piece of complexity in that stack, and it buys exactly one scenario.

Also deliberately absent: the fast presence covariance (so on/off detection is reactive rather than anticipatory), two-component polarization, and the frequency/notch layer. Each is recorded with the measurement it costs, and each is a self-contained addition.

15

The obvious fix is not the fix

Would a proper tracking filter close that gap?

The full implementation used a constant-velocity Kalman filter: it keeps a running estimate of the jammer's angle and its rate of change, blending each new measurement against its own prediction. We measured what it could buy before considering building one.

The aim is angle + rate × mean age. A filter can only improve the rate. So feed the loop the true rate — that bounds any rate estimator that could ever exist. Feed it the true angle too, and you learn which half is actually binding.

A perfect rate estimator is worth at most 1.5 dB — so the filter is not the answer. Eight dB sits in knowing where the jammer is, at most 1.5 dB in knowing how fast it moves. Given the right angle, the simple two-point null already matches the perfect-knowledge solution to within 0.3 dB. Nothing is wrong with the beamformer.

The angle's lag is set by the memory constant, not by a predictor. Shortening it is worth +8.3 dB at 1°/s — but it breaks the on/off case, because a short memory loses the jammer while it is off and the tracker then chases noise. The fix for that is the fast presence detector already on the deferred list. Order of work: presence detector, then shorter memory. The Kalman filter is not on the path.

16

Why this version exists

Two tuned numbers, and everything else derived

FORGETTING_LAMBDA = 0.90; % how far back the covariance remembers COVARIANCE_HORIZON_STEPS = 10; % = 1/(1−λ) — window length, sets memory ESTIMATE_LAG_STEPS = 9; % = λ/(1−λ) — mean age, sets the lead LOADING_FACTOR = 30; % × noise floor; set by measurement

Everything else follows from something: K = 2N from Reed–Mallett–Brennan; the guard from the array's beamwidth; the source threshold from the Marchenko–Pastur edge; run lengths from the estimators' convergence requirements; the lead from λ; the lead cap from the guard.

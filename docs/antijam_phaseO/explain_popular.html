Anti-jam beamforming · a plain-language guide

How an antenna array
learns to ignore a jammer

There is a signal you want and a hostile transmitter drowning it out. Nobody tells you where the jammer is. You have a few seconds and a handful of antennas. This is how the system works out what to do — and where, honestly, it still struggles.

The problem, in one picture

An array is several antenna elements listening at once. Each hears the same world, but from a slightly different position — so a wave arriving from one direction reaches them at slightly different moments.

That tiny timing difference is the whole trick. If you delay each element by just the right amount before adding them up, waves from your chosen direction line up and reinforce, while waves from elsewhere arrive out of step and partly cancel. You have pointed the array without moving it.

Two things matter and they are not the same. A beam is where you are listening hardest. A null is a direction you are almost perfectly deaf to. You want the beam on your signal and a null on the jammer, and — this is the hard part — you must place the null without being told where the jammer is.

The classical trick: don't look for the jammer at all

The natural instinct is to find the jammer first, then null it. The classical method does something cleverer, and it is the foundation of everything here.

Instead, tell the array: keep exactly unit sensitivity toward my signal's direction, and subject to that, make the total noise coming out of you as small as you possibly can.

That single instruction solves the problem by itself. The array cannot reduce your signal — it is pinned. So the only way to get quieter is to become deaf toward whatever else is loud. If a jammer is blaring from somewhere, the cheapest way to go quiet is to grow a null right at it. Nobody had to find it.

Minimise what comes out, while holding the wanted direction fixed. The jammer gets nulled as a side effect of the array trying to be quiet.

The array works this out from a running summary of what it is hearing — which directions currently carry energy and how they relate to one another. That summary is called the covariance, and it is the only thing the algorithm is allowed to see. It never gets told the jammer's position.

What "listening" actually means here

Every 50 milliseconds the receiver hands the algorithm a fresh block of samples from every element. It folds them into its running summary, recomputes the best weights, and applies them. Twenty times a second, forever.

The summary is faded as it goes: recent samples count fully, older ones are gradually forgotten. That fading is what lets the array adapt to a jammer that moves — and, as it turns out, it is also the source of most of the problems in this report.

Why fading memory causes trouble

How fast should the array forget? It is a genuine dilemma with no free answer.

Forget slowly and the summary is smooth and reliable — but it describes where the jammer was, not where it is. Forget quickly and you track a moving jammer, but the summary becomes jittery and the null gets sloppy.

The system forgets over about half a second. Measured against a drifting jammer, the array behaves precisely like something aiming half a second behind the target — and against a jammer sweeping at two degrees per second, that is a whole degree of aiming error. Enough to miss.

That is what the motion tracker does. It watches the estimated jammer direction over time, works out its speed, and aims the null where the jammer will be a fraction of a second from now. The amount to lead by is not guesswork: it is the memory lag plus the small delay in applying new weights, which comes out at about 0.7 seconds of lead.

On a drifting jammer this took the system from tracking the theoretical best 45% of the time to 90%. And because it only engages when the jammer is actually moving, standing-still cases are completely unaffected — not "barely affected", but identical to the last decimal.

The jammer that blinks

A cleverer adversary does not transmit continuously. It pulses — a few seconds on, a few seconds off — hoping you waste your effort re-learning it each time it returns.

There is a genuine opportunity here. If the pulsing is regular, you can learn the rhythm and have the null already in place the moment it comes back, instead of spending the first half-second of every burst catching up.

The system had exactly that feature. Testing it revealed something uncomfortable: it had essentially never worked. Across every test it engaged on at most 1.3% of the time steps, and in most conditions never at all.

When the pulses are slow

To spot a rhythm you need to see several repeats. The system kept a memory of the last 51 seconds and insisted on three full cycles inside it — so any pattern slower than about 17 seconds could never be recognised, no matter how obvious.

Not a tuning problem. Just arithmetic nobody had checked.

When the pulses are fast

This one is subtler and more interesting. To spot the rhythm, the system first has to notice "jammer on" and "jammer off". But it read that from the same fading summary the beamformer uses — and that summary is deliberately smoothed.

With a half-second memory and a one-second gap, the echo of the last burst was still in the summary when the next arrived. It concluded the jammer was on always. A flat signal has no rhythm to find.

One setting was quietly doing two incompatible jobs. The beamformer wants a long memory to stay steady. The blink-detector wants a short one to see the edges. You cannot have both from one number — so we gave the detector a second, faster memory of its own.

With its own fast memory, the detector's estimate of how often the jammer is actually transmitting went from being wrong by 46 percentage points to wrong by 13, and the anticipatory feature started firing for the first time.

A mistake worth showing you

Fixing the detector exposed something that had been hidden behind it, and the story is a good illustration of how this kind of work actually goes.

Once the system could tell the jammer had stopped, it did the apparently sensible thing: dropped the null and went back to listening at full sensitivity. Why keep a deaf spot pointed at something that is not transmitting?

Because you have to rebuild the null when the jammer returns, and rebuilding costs about half a second. If the gap was only two seconds long, you spend a quarter of every cycle recovering from a decision that was supposed to help.

Two confident explanations, both wrong

The obvious suspects were that the detector's timing was off, or that the anticipatory feature was firing too eagerly. Both were tested by adjusting the relevant setting across its range.

In both cases the score did not move at all — it stayed at exactly 80.1 while the knob swung across its whole range. That is the useful signal: when a number refuses to budge as you change something, you are changing the wrong thing. The cause was elsewhere entirely.

So a gentler policy was built: instead of dropping the null the instant the jammer goes quiet, relax it gradually, so a brief gap barely loosens it while a long one lets the array fully recover its sensitivity. It sounds obviously better.

It was worse. Measured across ninety scenarios it scored 76 against the blunt policy's 81, and lost in the majority of cases. It did rescue the handful of situations it was designed for — it just charged far more for that rescue everywhere else.

Why it looked good before it was tested properly

The gentle policy had been tuned on five hand-picked "hard cases" — the situations where the blunt policy struggled. Three of the five were problem cases.

But in the real population, problem cases are eleven out of ninety. The tuning set was 60% problems where reality is 12%. It was optimised for a world that does not exist, and it duly won there and lost everywhere else.

The negative result turned out to be the valuable part. If you imagine a magical version that somehow picks the better of the two policies for every single situation — something no real system could do, since it would require knowing the answer in advance — it scores 81.5 against the blunt policy's 80.6.

That is the entire remaining prize: nine tenths of one point, and not a single extra scenario brought up to standard. So the question of when to drop the null is closed. No amount of cleverness there is worth having. The effort belongs elsewhere.

Judging fairly across very different antennas

The system was tested on seven arrays, from a single antenna up to twenty. This creates an obvious scoring problem: a twenty-element array can do things a one-element array physically cannot. Grading them against a common target would just rank them by size.

So each is graded against its own ceiling — the best any perfect method could achieve on that particular antenna in that particular situation. The question is not "did it hit some number", but "did it get what this hardware had to give".

The single-element result is the one that convinced us the grading was right. One antenna has no ability to prefer one direction over another — nulling is not merely hard, it is meaningless. The system scored 100 because it did as well as anything could, which was nothing at all, and it said so instead of crashing.

That matters, because two of these arrays used to crash the software outright. Not perform badly — abort. A feature that was supposed to be optional refused to start on small antennas, and took the basic method down with it. Now they run, and the ones that genuinely cannot do the job report why.

The trap in this kind of scoring

A two-element array scores 99. A sixteen-element array scores 72. The small one is not better.

The two-element array's ceiling is low, so reaching it is easy. The sixteen-element array could deliver three times the performance, and we are getting less of what is available. A high score means "we extracted what was there" — never "this is a good antenna". Both numbers have to be read together, always.

An antenna that cannot tell up from down

One array turned out to be physically incapable of distinguishing a direction from its mirror image below the horizon. Not imprecise — exactly incapable. The two directions produce identical readings, to the limits of measurement.

This is not a defect. It is the expected behaviour of a flat array of simple antennas: with nothing to break the symmetry, up and down genuinely look the same. Every flat array has some version of it.

It has a curious consequence. For nulling, it does not matter at all — if the two directions are indistinguishable, a null aimed at the mirror is automatically a null aimed at the real thing. But for tracking it is ruinous: the estimate flips between the two, and a tracker measuring speed sees the jammer teleporting back and forth.

So the tracker measures each antenna's symmetry when it starts up and, where the ambiguity is real, quietly keeps its estimates on one consistent side. With that, the direction estimates on that array went from being wrong by 139 degrees to wrong by 1.5. They had been right all along — just reported from the wrong hemisphere.

What actually limits this system

After all of the above, the thing most likely to stop this working in the field is none of it. It is calibration.

Everything depends on knowing precisely how each element responds — a fraction of a wavelength of error in the assumed geometry and the array is solving a slightly wrong problem. Worse, it fails in a nasty direction: the method tries to minimise output power, and if it is mistaken about where your signal is, the cheapest way to go quiet is to null your own signal.

Measured: with per-element phase errors of about one degree it still works. By three degrees every method is at the floor, and the spread between builds is enormous — one unit fine, the next unusable. That belongs in the hardware specification, and it is a bigger risk than any algorithm choice discussed here.

Where it stands

Works well: a stationary jammer, and a pulsing one, on every array tested, once the detector was given its own memory. Slow drift is handled by aiming ahead.

Works partly: fast drift. Beyond roughly ten degrees a second the prediction extends the envelope but does not remove the limit.

Not yet addressed: calibration tolerance in the hardware spec, more than one jammer at a time, and the computational cost of the direction-finding step, which is currently far too expensive for real-time hardware.

The habit underneath all of this

Three times in this work a confident explanation turned out to be wrong, and each time the same thing exposed it: a number that refused to move when the supposed cause was adjusted.

And once, a recommendation was issued on a measurement already known to be compromised — the tests were too short for the feature being judged, which had been written down as a caveat and then not acted on. A longer re-run reversed the conclusion entirely.

The pattern worth taking away is not any particular fix. It is that the measurement design decided the answer at least as often as the algorithm design did.
